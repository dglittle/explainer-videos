notes, and let him choose. My notes (verify them yourself):
- Mostly right. The "funny" classical step is a 2^n-long one-hot vector times a 0/1 matrix with exactly one 1 per column.
  The machine's state should also include the head position and the control state, not just the tape (or fold them into the tape).
- Complex numbers: yes, |z|² = z·z̄ (z times its complex conjugate), and the length is sqrt(Σ|a_i|²). He's right.
- "Matrix keeps the length 1 for every input" = the matrix is UNITARY (U†U = I). The subtle bit: unitary ⇒ invertible, so the
  classical update must be REVERSIBLE: a permutation matrix. A normal TM step can erase information (two states → one), which isn't
  allowed. Fix: Bennett (1973) / Toffoli showed any computation can be made reversible with some extra scratch tape. So the classical
  steps that "survive" are exactly the permutation matrices, and quantum = all unitaries. Probably worth one optional line or an on-screen note.
- Missing piece: measurement. At the end you look, and you see state i with probability |a_i|² (the Born rule). That's WHY the
  length must be 1. Offer it as an optional line.
- Optional: the giant matrix is in practice a product of small local gates (each touches a few cells), like the TM head being local.
- Related ideas Greg might like (an optional credit, like the Falk's scheme credit in matmul): Scott Aaronson, "quantum mechanics is
  probability theory with the 2-norm and complex numbers" (Quantum Computing Since Democritus, lecture 9): the classical
  PROBABILISTIC version is a vector of non-negative numbers that sum to 1 (1-norm) and stochastic matrices; quantum swaps that for the 2-norm and
  complex numbers. Formal quantum Turing machines: Deutsch 1985, Bernstein–Vazirani 1993.

Greg's request, verbatim:
"I'd like to give my understanding of quantum computation, for someone who knows about regular computation. So, with regular computation, we can do it all with a Turing machine, which has a long tape of 0's and 1's. To simply things, let's assume the tape is finite. Then we could do a step of computation in a rather funny way.. we could list all the states the tape could possibly be in.. which is a lot, 2 to the power of however many cells.. and we can imagine a vector that long, with 0's everywhere, except a single one at the position representing the current state of the Turing machine tape. And then to apply the next state update, we multiple this vector by a giant square matrix, such that the resulting vector has a single one at the position representing the new state of the tape after the update. Now to make this quantum computation, all we need to do is, instead of having 0's everywhere with a single 1, we instead relax the requiement to say that the length of the vector must be 1 (which is the same as saying that the sum of the squares of all the elements must be 1). Of course, actually the numbers can be complex too, and rather than squaring them, we want to get the magnitude by taking the complex conjugate (if that's correct). And we also require the matrix to be such that the result will also have a magnitude of 1. And this vector times matrix setup is sort of the Turing machine of quantum computation. Any quantum computation boils down to this." ? thanks!!
